# Skill: User Interview

## Purpose
Extract real, specific information about how people experience a problem today — their current workflow, where it breaks down, what they've tried, and what would make them abandon a new solution. The goal is evidence, not validation. A good interview surfaces things that challenge the brief as often as it confirms it.

## When to Apply
- **At discovery:** before producing personas, journey maps, or a Problem Statement — always interview before forming a direction
- **During build:** when a sprint surfaces unexpected user behaviour or a spec assumption looks wrong
- **Post-launch:** when analytics signal a problem and you need qualitative context to understand why

## Setup

Before the interview:
1. State the specific questions you're trying to answer — not what you hope to confirm
2. Set context for the participant: "I'm not testing you, I'm trying to understand your experience"
3. Record or take notes in real time — do not rely on memory

## Core Questions

### Opening — Establish context
- Walk me through how you currently handle [the problem area]. What does a typical session look like?
- When did you last run into this problem? Can you walk me through exactly what happened?

### Depth — Understand the real workflow
- What tool or process do you use today to solve this?
- Where does that break down for you?
- What do you do when it breaks down?
- What have you already tried? Why didn't it stick?

### Stakes — Understand motivation
- How often does this happen? What does it cost you when it goes wrong?
- Who else is affected when this breaks?
- What would it change for you if this problem were solved?

### Abandonment — Understand risk
- What would make you go back to your old solution after two weeks with a new one?
- What would have to go wrong for you to stop using something new?

### Timing — Understand urgency
- Why is this a problem worth solving now, rather than a year ago?
- Is something changing in your context that makes this more pressing?

## Follow-up Probes

Use these when an answer is too abstract:
- "Can you give me a specific example?"
- "What did that actually look like?"
- "How did you feel when that happened?"
- "What did you do next?"

## Red Flags to Watch For

| Signal | What it means |
|--------|--------------|
| User describes a solution, not a problem | Redirect: "Tell me about the situation that led you to want that." |
| Answer is hypothetical ("I would probably...") | Redirect: "Tell me about the last time this actually happened." |
| User gives short yes/no answers | You're asking leading questions — open up: "Tell me more about that." |
| User agrees with everything | They're being polite, not honest — ask "What's missing?" or "What would you change?" |

## Output

After the interview, produce:
- **Key quotes** — verbatim, with context
- **Workflow summary** — how they actually solve the problem today, in their words
- **Pain points** — specific moments of friction, with severity signals
- **Surprises** — anything that contradicts the brief or a prior assumption
- **Open questions** — things the interview raised but didn't resolve

These feed directly into persona development, the journey map, and the assumption map.

## Notes
- One interview is evidence. Five interviews are a pattern. Do not form strong conclusions from a single session.
- The goal is not to hear what you want to hear. Surface contradictions and anomalies — they are the most valuable findings.
- "I want a faster horse" is a famous example of a user who can describe pain but not solution. Listen for the pain, not the proposed fix.
